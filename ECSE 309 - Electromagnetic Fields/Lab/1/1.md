## Introduction

We decided we first wanted to design the motor for speed before tackling aesthetics. We assumed that this would mean that we wanted to maximize vertical and outward pointing wire, as this should theoretically maximize torque. But as we found during this Lab was that the stability of the motor was greatly impacted by maximizing for these, and thus settled for a different solution in practice.

## Experimental Progress

The region around the magnets of the motor design we settled on has one circle coming from each side rotated in such a way that current is flowing the same way in both circles. The circles of wire have a diameter of $17.6~\text{mm}$ and the magnets have a diameter of $12~\text{mm}$ meaning that the wire is on average $2.8~\text{mm}$ away from the surface of the magnets. The entire design with measurements can be seen in figure 0, and the force exerted on the wires around the magnets can be seen in figure 3.3.

Equation 1 is the equation for resistance of a wire where $L$ is length of wire, $\rho$ is resistivity of the material the wire is made of, and $A$ is cross sectional area of the wire.

$$
\begin{align}
R=\frac{\rho L}{A}\tag{1}\\
V=IR\tag{2}\\
\end{align}
$$

The total length of the wire is $257.4~\text{mm}$; resistivity of copper is $1.68 * 10^{-5}~\ohm~\text{mm}$; and the diameter of our wire is $0.9~\text{mm}$ meaning the cross sectional area is $0.636~\text{mm}^2$. By inputting these values into equation 1 the resistance of the wire is calculated to be $0.0068 ~\ohm$. The internal resistance of the given AA battery is $0.2~\ohm$ and the contact resistance between the washer and the battery is $0.1~\ohm$ meaning that our total resistance is $0.3069~\ohm$. Now using Ohms law (Eq. 2) and the knowledge that the voltage across the battery is $1.5~V$. The maximum current across the wire in the case of a shorting of the two terminals is $4.89~A$; however the maximum current the battery can support is $2.5-3~A$ so all further calculations will be done assuming $2.5~A$.

The wires around the magnets were modeled in `matlab` using a script which converted length down the wire into position of the wire. This model can be seen in figure 3.1 it models the two circles we have surrounding the magnets that are $0.7~\text{mm}$ apart from each other and have a $1.2~\text{mm}$ gap between the end and beginning of the arc as can be seen in figure 0. The value for the effects of the magnetic field along the path of the wire was obtained using the matlab code provided, can be found in the data tables below. This value is reasonable for the types of magnets being used along with the distance between the surface of the magnets and the wires.

In the same simulation as above torque was also calculated, and an image of the forces on the wire can be seen in figure 3.2. The torque of the final design was calculated to be $1.4462 * 10^{-9}~Nm$. Some of the previous iterations can be seen in figures 1 and 2, and their may also be found in the data table below. While not bad solutions the final one was definitely an improvement. A lot of other prototypes were tested in person by trying different ways of coiling the wire around the battery and magnets, but the 3 simulated paint a good picture to the evolution towards the final product. Figure 1.1 ran into a lot of stability issues in person due to the wires not circling the battery and thus being very susceptible to any slight imperfections in terms of balance, as well as performing slower in practice. Figure 2.1 ran into some of the same issues as Figure 1.1 when built. Ultimately the circle around the magnet method was decided upon because it made balancing the two sides relatively easy and also provided very promising results in terms of speed of rotation with an rpm of 480 (take this number with a grain of salt; we used a low end estimate based off of a slowed down video of our motor rotation).
